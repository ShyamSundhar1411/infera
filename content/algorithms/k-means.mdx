## The idea

Imagine opening k coffee shops in a town. Each resident walks to the nearest shop. Then each shop moves to the middle of its own customers. Some residents now have a closer shop, so they switch, and the shops move again.

Eventually nobody switches and the shops stop moving. That settled state is the clustering. It is a good one, but not always the best possible one: it depends on where the shops started.

## How it learns

1. Choose k starting centres, either random points or spread out with k-means++.
2. Assign every point to its nearest centre.
3. Move each centre to the mean of the points assigned to it.
4. Repeat until no point changes cluster.

## Reading the picture

Each point is coloured by its cluster, and the large markers are the centres. The background shows which centre owns each region of the plane, forming straight-edged cells halfway between neighbouring centres.

Step through training to see the two phases alternate. Inertia in the stats is the total squared distance from points to their centres; it can only go down.

## Try this

1. On **Blobs**, set Starting centres to Random points and Clusters (k) to 3. Reset a few times: sometimes two centres end up sharing one blob.
2. Switch to k-means++ and reset again. Spreading the starts out makes bad outcomes much rarer.
3. Load **Moons**. K-means cuts the moons with a straight line because it only understands round, compact groups.
4. On **Blobs**, slide Clusters (k) from 1 to 6 and note the Inertia each time. Look for the elbow where adding clusters stops helping much.

## The maths

<Formulas>

<Formula label="Assign">

$$
c_i = \arg\min_j \lVert x_i - \mu_j \rVert^2
$$

</Formula>

<Formula label="Update">

$$
\mu_j = \frac{1}{\lvert S_j \rvert} \sum_{i \in S_j} x_i
$$

</Formula>

<Formula label="Inertia">

$$
J = \sum_i \lVert x_i - \mu_{c_i} \rVert^2
$$

</Formula>

<Formula label="k-means++">

$$
P(\text{choose } x) \propto D(x)^2, \quad D(x) = \text{distance to nearest chosen centre}
$$

</Formula>

</Formulas>

## In a real system

Vector databases use k-means to split millions of embeddings into clusters ahead of time, so a search only has to look inside the few clusters nearest the query.

## Strengths and weaknesses

<ProsCons>
<Card>

### Good at

- Simple, fast and scales to large datasets.
- Easy to explain and to visualise.
- Always converges, usually in a few steps.

</Card>
<Card>

### Struggles with

- You must choose k in advance.
- Assumes round clusters of similar size.
- Results depend on the starting centres.

</Card>
</ProsCons>
