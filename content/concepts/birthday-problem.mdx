## The idea

How many people do you need in a room before two of them probably share a birthday? Most people guess somewhere around 180, half of 365. The answer is 23.

The intuition goes wrong because it imagines matching your own birthday. With 23 people you are not making 22 comparisons, you are making 253: every person against every other. Each pair is unlikely to match, but there are a lot of pairs, and the number grows roughly like $n^2/2$.

The same maths decides how soon two items land in the same slot of a hash table, how many random IDs you can issue before two collide, and the "birthday attack" on cryptographic hashes. With $d$ equally likely values, a collision becomes likely after about $\sqrt{d}$ draws, far sooner than $d$.

## How to think about it

1. It is easier to compute the chance that nobody matches.
2. The second person must avoid one birthday, the third must avoid two, and so on.
3. Multiply those chances together: $\frac{364}{365} \cdot \frac{363}{365} \cdots$
4. Each factor is close to 1, but there are many of them, and they shrink.
5. One minus that product is the chance of at least one match. It crosses 50% at 23 people and 99% at 57.

## The maths

<Formulas>

<Formula label="No match among n people">

$$
P(\text{no match}) = \prod_{k=0}^{n-1} \left(1 - \frac{k}{d}\right)
$$

</Formula>

<Formula label="At least one match">

$$
P(\text{match}) = 1 - \frac{d!}{(d - n)!\, d^{\,n}}
$$

</Formula>

<Formula label="Pairs to compare">

$$
\binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n - 1)}{2}
$$

</Formula>

<Formula label="Approximation">

$$
P(\text{match}) \approx 1 - e^{-n(n-1)/(2d)}
$$

</Formula>

<Formula label="People needed for an even chance">

$$
n_{50} \approx \sqrt{2 d \ln 2} \approx 1.18\sqrt{d}
$$

</Formula>

</Formulas>
