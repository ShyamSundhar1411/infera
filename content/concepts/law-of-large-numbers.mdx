## The idea

Roll a die once and you might get a 6. Average ten rolls and you will probably be somewhere between 2.5 and 4.5. Average ten thousand and you will be within a whisker of 3.5. Individual outcomes stay random, but their average becomes predictable.

Each line on the chart is one person rolling and keeping a running average. Early on the lines swing wildly. As $n$ grows they all squeeze together onto the true mean.

## How fast?

The spread of the average shrinks like $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$. To halve the error you need four times as many draws; to get one more decimal place you need a hundred times as many. The shaded band shows $\mu \pm 2\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, where about 95% of the averages should sit at each $n$.

## When it fails

The law needs the expected value to exist. The Cauchy distribution (Student's $t$ with $\nu = 1$) has tails so heavy that its mean is undefined. Its running averages never settle: every so often a single huge draw yanks the average somewhere new.

## Try this

1. Run the **Fair die** with 10 paths. Notice how wild the first 20 rolls are and how tight things look by 1,000.
2. Switch to **Exponential**. The paths overshoot upwards more than downwards early on because the distribution is skewed, but they still converge.
3. Pick **Cauchy** and run it several times. The paths jump and never settle, because there is no mean for them to converge to.

## The maths

<Formulas>

<Formula label="Sample mean">

$$
\bar{X}_n = \frac{X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n}{n}
$$

</Formula>

<Formula label="Weak law">

$$
P\left(\lvert \bar{X}_n - \mu \rvert > \varepsilon\right) \to 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty, \text{ for every } \varepsilon > 0
$$

</Formula>

<Formula label="Strong law">

$$
P\left(\bar{X}_n \to \mu\right) = 1
$$

</Formula>

<Formula label="Spread of the average">

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}, \qquad \operatorname{SD}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

</Formula>

<Formula label="Chebyshev bound">

$$
P\left(\lvert \bar{X}_n - \mu \rvert \ge \varepsilon\right) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n \varepsilon^2}
$$

</Formula>

</Formulas>
