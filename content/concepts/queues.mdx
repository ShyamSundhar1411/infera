## The idea

A desk that can serve 60 people an hour, with 54 people arriving an hour, sounds comfortable: it is only 90% busy. But people do not arrive on a timetable. Sometimes three turn up in the same minute, sometimes nobody comes for five. When a burst arrives the line grows, and the desk only has 10% of its time spare to work the line back down.

That is the whole story of queueing theory. Averages tell you whether a system can keep up in the long run. Randomness tells you how long the lines get on the way.

## How to think about it

1. Arrivals are random and independent: the gaps between them are Exponential, so the count per minute is Poisson (see the arrivals lab on the [Poisson page](/distributions/poisson/)).
2. Each service also takes a random time.
3. Utilisation $\rho$ is the arrival rate divided by what the desks can handle. Below 1 the line keeps draining; at 1 or above it grows forever.
4. With one desk, the average time in the system is $1/(\mu - \lambda)$: it depends on the spare capacity, not the busy share. Halve the spare capacity and the wait doubles.
5. Little's law ties it together: the average number of people in the system equals the arrival rate times the average time each one spends there.

## Reading the picture

The coloured dots are people waiting; the rings fill as each desk serves someone. The small line underneath tracks the length of the queue. The chart shows the theory for the average time from arriving to leaving, against how busy the desks are, with your setting marked and the simulated average as a dot. ## Try this

1. Start at 50% busy and watch: short lines that clear quickly. Then move **How busy the desks are** to 90%. Same desk, same people, but the line now wanders off to ten or twenty and takes ages to recover.
2. At 95%, press **Skip ahead 5,000 minutes** and compare the average time in the system with the theory. It is around 20 minutes for a 1-minute job.
3. Keep 90% busy and set **Desks** to 3. Each desk is just as busy as before, yet waits shrink a lot, because a burst can be shared between three desks instead of piling onto one.
4. Check Little's law in the stats: the average number in the system matches arrivals per minute times the average time spent, whatever the settings.

## In real systems

Every server, database connection pool and message queue is one of these desks. This is why teams keep machines well below full load, why a small traffic increase can suddenly double latency, and why retries during an incident are so dangerous: they push utilisation over 1, and the queue never drains.

The scene below puts one of these desks inside a web service. Drag the visitors slider, or switch on the second server, and watch where the line forms.

```topple caption="One app server between visitors and a database. The waits in the log are the single-desk formula: 10, 40, 190 and 990 ms."
title: Why a busy server's queue explodes
summary: A server that handles 100 requests a second is quick at 50, slower at 80 and miserable at 95. Requests arrive at random, so waiting time does not grow in a straight line; it blows up as the server nears its limit.
kit: outage
duration: 46s

state busy "Busy, short queue" tone=warn reject=0
state swamped "Long queue" tone=hot reject=0

node users "Visitors" icon=users
node app "App server" icon=server capacity=100
node db "Database" icon=database capacity=400

users -> app "requests" rate=50
app -> db "queries" rate=50

slider traffic "Visitors per second" users -> app rate 20..130

chapter 0s "Half busy" tag="50% load"
  Each request takes the server 10 milliseconds of work, so it can finish
  100 a second. Fifty arrive each second, but not on a schedule: they come
  in random clumps, and a request that lands during a clump waits its turn.

unless second
  at 1s: app log info "load 50% · average wait in line 10 ms"

  chapter 8s "A busier afternoon" tag="80% load"
    Traffic rises to 80 a second. Load went up by 60%, but the average wait
    quadruples: there is less spare time between clumps to clear the line.

  at 8s: users -> app rate=x1.6 "80 requests a second"
  at 8s: app -> db rate=x1.6
  at 8.5s: app busy "Queue forming in bursts"
  at 9s: app log info "load 80% · average wait in line 40 ms"

  chapter 16s "Nearly full" tag="95% load"
    At 95 a second the server is busy 95% of the time. Any burst takes
    ages to drain, because only 5% of its time is free to catch up. The
    average wait is now nineteen times the work itself.

  at 16s: users -> app rate=x1.9 "95 requests a second"
  at 16s: app -> db rate=x1.9
  at 16.5s: app swamped "Rarely catches up"
  at 17s: app log warn "load 95% · average wait in line 190 ms"
  at 22s: users -> app rate=x1.98 "99 requests a second"
  at 22s: app -> db rate=x1.98
  at 22.5s: app log error "load 99% · average wait in line 990 ms"

  chapter 26s "Over the limit"
    At 110 a second the line can never empty. It grows until it is full,
    and then the server turns requests away.

  at 26s: users -> app rate=x2.2 "110 requests a second"
  at 26s: app -> db rate=x2
  at 27s: app log error "queue full: rejecting requests"
  at 34s: users -> app rate=x1.4 "Traffic falls back to 70"
  at 34s: app -> db rate=x1.4
  at 36s: app busy
  at 37s: app log info "load 70% · average wait in line 23 ms"

option second "Add a second app server"
  node app2 "Second app server" icon=server capacity=100
  users -> app2 "requests" rate=0
  app2 -> db "queries" rate=0
  at 0.5s: users -> app2 rate=25 "Half the traffic goes to the new server"
  at 0.5s: users -> app rate=25

  chapter 8s "Two servers share the line" tag="What if"
    The same traffic now has two servers to choose from. At 80 a second
    each is 40% busy, and a request only waits when both are busy at once.

  at 8s: users -> app rate=40 "80 a second in total"
  at 8s: users -> app2 rate=40
  at 9s: app log info "load 40% each · average wait in line 2 ms"

  chapter 16s "Plenty of slack"
    At 95 a second, where one server made people wait 190 ms, two servers
    keep the average wait under 3 ms. Halving the load did not halve the
    wait; it cut it by a factor of sixty.

  at 16s: users -> app rate=47.5 "95 a second in total"
  at 16s: users -> app2 rate=47.5
  at 17s: app log info "load 47.5% each · average wait in line 3 ms"

chapter 40s "What it teaches"
  Waiting grows like load divided by spare capacity, load / (1 - load):
  1 at 50%, 4 at 80%, 19 at 95%, 99 at 99%. For anything people wait on,
  plan to run well below the limit, around 70%, and add capacity before the
  busy season, not during it.
```

Two more places where chance shapes a system. When one request fans out to many servers, a rare slow server becomes the common case. And when a load balancer places work at random, the busiest server ends up far above the average, unless it checks two servers before choosing.

```topple caption="Fan-out to 100 shards: each is slow 1% of the time, so 63% of searches wait for a slow one. Hedged requests fix most of it."
title: Why the slowest server sets your speed
summary: One search asks 100 servers and waits for all of them. Each is slow only 1% of the time, yet most searches end up waiting for a slow one.
kit: agents
traffic: off
duration: 40s

state slow "Paused, answering late" tone=hot reject=0

node user "You" icon=user
node front "Search front end" icon=server
group index "Search index, 100 shards"
  node s1 "Shard 1" icon=database
  node s2 "Shard 2" icon=database
  node s3 "Shard 3" icon=database
  node rest "Shards 4 to 100" icon=cluster

user <-> front
front <-> s1
front <-> s2
front <-> s3
front <-> rest

chapter 0s "One search, a hundred servers" tag="Fan-out"
  The index is too big for one machine, so it is split into 100 shards. A
  search goes to every shard, and the front end can only answer once all
  100 have replied.

at 1s: user -> front send "A search"
at 1.5s: front waiting "Waiting for 100 answers"
at 2s: front -> s1 send
at 2s: front -> s2 send
at 2s: front -> s3 send
at 2s: front -> rest send count=6 "The same query, 97 more times"
at 2.5s: s1 working
at 2.5s: s2 working
at 2.5s: s3 working
at 2.5s: rest working
at 3.5s: s1 -> front send "10 ms"
at 3.5s: s2 -> front send "11 ms"
at 3.5s: s3 -> front send "9 ms"
at 3.5s: rest -> front send count=6 "97 answers, all quick"
at 4s: s1 idle
at 4s: s2 idle
at 4s: s3 idle
at 4s: rest idle
at 4.5s: front -> user send "Results in 14 ms"
at 5s: front done
at 5s: front log info "search 1: 14 ms, slowest shard 11 ms"

chapter 8s "Fast almost always"
  Each shard answers in about 10 ms. Once in a hundred requests it is
  caught by a pause, such as garbage collection or a slow disk read, and
  takes a full second instead.

chapter 12s "But a search waits for the slowest" tag="The tail"
  The next search goes out the same way. This time shard 2 is in one of
  its pauses. The other 99 answer at once, and the whole search waits.

at 12s: front idle
at 12.5s: user -> front send "Another search"
at 13s: front waiting
at 13.5s: front -> s1 send
at 13.5s: front -> s2 send
at 13.5s: front -> s3 send
at 13.5s: front -> rest send count=6
at 14s: s1 working
at 14s: s2 slow "Garbage collection pause"
at 14s: s3 working
at 14s: rest working
at 15s: s1 -> front send "10 ms"
at 15s: s3 -> front send "9 ms"
at 15s: rest -> front send count=6 "Quick answers"
at 15.5s: s1 idle
at 15.5s: s3 idle
at 15.5s: rest idle

unless hedge
  at 16s: front log warn "search 2: still waiting on shard 2"
  at 21s: s2 -> front send "1,000 ms"
  at 21.5s: s2 idle
  at 22s: front -> user send "Results in 1,012 ms"
  at 22.5s: front done
  at 22.5s: front log error "search 2: 1,012 ms, slowest shard 1,000 ms"

option hedge "Hedge: ask a replica when a shard is late"
  node r2 "Shard 2 replica" icon=database
  front <-> r2
  at 16s: front -> r2 send "No answer after 15 ms: ask the replica"
  at 16.5s: r2 working
  at 17.5s: r2 -> front send "10 ms"
  at 18s: r2 idle
  at 18s: front -> user send "Results in 27 ms"
  at 18.5s: front done
  at 18.5s: front log info "search 2: 27 ms, hedged around shard 2"

  chapter 16s "Ask twice, take the first" tag="What if"
    The front end does not wait. If a shard has not answered after 15 ms,
    the usual time for nearly all of them, it sends the same query to a
    replica and uses whichever answer arrives first. Both copies being
    slow happens once in 10,000 requests, so slow searches drop from 63%
    to about 1%, for a few percent of extra load.

chapter 26s "The arithmetic" tag="1 - 0.99^100"
  The chance that all 100 shards are quick is 0.99 multiplied by itself
  100 times, about 37%. So 63% of searches meet at least one slow shard.
  A one-in-a-hundred problem on each server becomes the usual experience
  for the person searching.

at 26s: front log info "P(at least one slow shard) = 1 - 0.99^100 = 63%"

chapter 34s "What it teaches"
  When a request fans out, the tail of each server's latency becomes the
  middle of yours. Measure the 99th percentile, not the average, and fix
  it by hedging, by fanning out to fewer servers, or by removing the pauses.
```

```topple caption="Random placement against the best of two random servers, for 1,000 jobs on 1,000 servers."
title: The power of two choices
summary: Send each job to a random server and some servers drown while others sit idle. Look at two random servers and pick the less busy one, and the pile-ups almost vanish.
kit: outage
duration: 40s

node users "Jobs" icon=users
node lb "Load balancer" icon=lb
group fleet "1,000 servers"
  node s1 "Server 17" icon=server capacity=30
  node s2 "Server 402" icon=server capacity=30
  node s3 "Server 655" icon=server capacity=30
  node s4 "Server 931" icon=server capacity=30
  node rest "The other 996" icon=cluster

users -> lb "long jobs" rate=60
lb -> s1 rate=12
lb -> s2 rate=12
lb -> s3 rate=12
lb -> s4 rate=12
lb -> rest rate=12

chapter 0s "Even on average" tag="1 job each"
  A thousand long-running jobs arrive for a thousand servers: one each on
  average. Each server can comfortably run two or three at once.

unless two
  chapter 6s "Pick a server at random" tag="Random"
    The load balancer sends every job to a server chosen at random. Random
    is fair on average, but not in each place: some servers are picked
    again and again, and about 37% are never picked at all.

  at 6s: lb log info "strategy: one random server per job"
  at 8s: lb -> s1 rate=36 "Picked 6 times"
  at 8s: lb -> s3 rate=0 "Never picked"
  at 8s: lb -> s4 rate=6
  at 9s: s1 overloaded "6 jobs, room for 3"
  at 9.5s: s3 offline "Idle"
  at 10s: s1 log error "6 jobs queued, each running at half speed"
  at 11s: lb log warn "busiest server: 6 jobs | idle servers: 37%"

  chapter 18s "The busiest server sets the pace"
    The job stuck on server 17 finishes last, and with a thousand servers
    the busiest one typically ends up with 5 or 6 jobs. Adding servers
    barely helps: the worst pile grows slowly, like log n over log log n.

option two "Look at two servers, pick the less busy"
  chapter 6s "Two random choices" tag="What if"
    For each job the load balancer picks two servers at random, asks how
    busy each is, and sends the job to the quieter one. One extra question
    per job.

  at 6s: lb log info "strategy: best of two random servers"
  at 8s: lb -> s1 rate=18 "3 jobs"
  at 8s: lb -> s3 rate=6 "1 job"
  at 8s: lb -> s4 rate=12 "2 jobs"
  at 9s: s1 healthy "3 jobs, within capacity"
  at 11s: lb log info "busiest server: 3 jobs | idle servers: 24%"

  chapter 18s "The pile-ups vanish"
    The busiest server now has 3 jobs instead of 5 or 6, and it stays near
    3 even as the fleet grows, because the worst pile grows like log log n.
    The second choice does nearly all the work; a third adds little.

chapter 30s "What it teaches"
  Randomness spreads load evenly on average but leaves hot spots. A
  little information, one extra sample per decision, removes most of
  them. Many real load balancers use exactly this rule.
```

$$

L_q = \frac{\rho^2}{1 - \rho}

<Formula label="Little's law">

L = \lambda W
