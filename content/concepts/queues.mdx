## The idea

A desk that can serve 60 people an hour, with 54 people arriving an hour, sounds comfortable: it is only 90% busy. But people do not arrive on a timetable. Sometimes three turn up in the same minute, sometimes nobody comes for five. When a burst arrives the line grows, and the desk only has 10% of its time spare to work the line back down.

That is the whole story of queueing theory. Averages tell you whether a system can keep up in the long run. Randomness tells you how long the lines get on the way.

## How to think about it

1. Arrivals are random and independent: the gaps between them are Exponential, so the count per minute is Poisson (see the arrivals lab on the [Poisson page](/distributions/poisson/)).
2. Each service also takes a random time.
3. Utilisation $\rho$ is the arrival rate divided by what the desks can handle. Below 1 the line keeps draining; at 1 or above it grows forever.
4. With one desk, the average time in the system is $1/(\mu - \lambda)$: it depends on the spare capacity, not the busy share. Halve the spare capacity and the wait doubles.
5. Little's law ties it together: the average number of people in the system equals the arrival rate times the average time each one spends there.

## Try this

1. Start at 50% busy and watch: short lines that clear quickly. Then move **How busy the desks are** to 90%. Same desk, same people, but the line now wanders off to ten or twenty and takes ages to recover.
2. At 95%, press **Skip ahead 5,000 minutes** and compare the average time in the system with the theory. It is around 20 minutes for a 1-minute job.
3. Keep 90% busy and set **Desks** to 3. Each desk is just as busy as before, yet waits shrink a lot, because a burst can be shared between three desks instead of piling onto one.
4. Check Little's law in the stats: the average number in the system matches arrivals per minute times the average time spent, whatever the settings.

## In real systems

Every server, database connection pool and message queue is one of these desks. This is why teams keep machines well below full load, why a small traffic increase can suddenly double latency, and why retries during an incident are so dangerous: they push utilisation over 1, and the queue never drains.

\rho = \frac{\lambda}{c\,\mu}

<Formula label="One desk: time in system">

$$
W = \frac{1}{\mu - \lambda} = \frac{1/\mu}{1 - \rho}

</Formula>

<Formula label="One desk: people waiting">

$$
L_q = \frac{\rho^2}{1 - \rho}

<Formula label="Little's law">

$$
L = \lambda W
$$
