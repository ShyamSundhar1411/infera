## The idea

Take any distribution: flat, skewed, U-shaped, even a coin flip. Draw $n$ values and average them. Do that thousands of times and histogram the averages. As $n$ grows, the histogram turns into a bell curve every time.

This is why the normal distribution is everywhere. Heights, measurement errors and test scores are sums of many small, independent effects, and sums behave like averages.

## Reading the picture

Each panel starts from a different source distribution, drawn small at the top. The histogram shows standardised averages, $z = (\bar{X}_n - \mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt{n})$, and the dashed curve is the standard normal $N(0, 1)$. Standardising removes the shrinking spread, so you can watch the shape alone converge.

Skewed sources like the exponential and the 20% coin take longer to look normal than symmetric ones like the uniform. Symmetric sources often look convincing by $n = 5$.

## How it relates to the law of large numbers

The law of large numbers says the average converges to $\mu$. The central limit theorem says how it is spread around $\mu$ on the way: normally, with width $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$. One gives the destination, the other the shape of the fluctuations.

## Try this

1. Start at $n = 1$. Each histogram is just the source distribution, standardised. Then slide $n$ up slowly and watch them all morph into the same bell.
2. Compare **Uniform** and **Exponential** at $n = 5$. The uniform already looks normal; the exponential is still leaning right.
3. Look at the **Coin ($p = 0.2$)** panel at small $n$. Averages of coin flips can only take a few values, so the histogram is spiky until $n$ gets large.

## The maths

<Formulas>

<Formula label="Standardised mean">

$$
Z_n = \frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}}
$$

</Formula>

<Formula label="The theorem">

$$
Z_n \to N(0, 1) \quad \text{in distribution as } n \to \infty
$$

</Formula>

<Formula label="Approximate form">

$$
\bar{X}_n \approx N\!\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right) \quad \text{for large } n
$$

</Formula>

<Formula label="Sums">

$$
X_1 + \cdots + X_n \approx N(n\mu, n\sigma^2)
$$

</Formula>

</Formulas>
