## The idea

You measure the heights of 10 people and get an average of 172 cm. The true average of everyone is surely not exactly 172, so you report a range instead: maybe 165 to 179. A confidence interval is a range like that, built by a recipe with a guarantee attached.

The guarantee is about the recipe, not about any one range. If you took many samples and built a 95% interval from each, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true average. Any single interval either contains it or it does not; you just do not know which.

## How it works

1. Take a sample of $n$ values and work out its mean $\bar{x}$ and standard deviation $s$.
2. The standard error $s/\sqrt{n}$ says how much sample means typically wobble.
3. Go a critical number of standard errors either side of $\bar{x}$: about 1.96 for 95% when $n$ is large.
4. For small samples, use the $t$ distribution's critical value instead. It is larger, which pays for the fact that $s$ is itself only an estimate.
5. Repeat with fresh samples, and about 95% of the intervals catch the true mean.

## What it does not mean

It is tempting to say "there is a 95% chance the true mean is in this interval". Strictly, it is not: the true mean is fixed, and this particular interval either contains it or not. The 95% describes how often the method works across repeated samples. A Bayesian credible interval is the tool that makes the "95% chance" statement, and it needs a prior.

## Reading the picture

Each horizontal line is one interval from one sample, with a dot at that sample's mean. The dashed line is the true mean, which in real life you would never see. Teal intervals cross it; coral ones miss. The bar underneath tracks the share of all intervals so far that caught the truth, against the confidence level promised.

## Try this

1. Press **Keep drawing** with the defaults. Roughly one interval in twenty misses, and the coverage bar settles near 95%.
2. Raise **Sample size (n)** from 10 to 100. Intervals get much narrower, yet coverage stays at 95%: more data buys precision, not a better hit rate.
3. Set **Sample size (n)** to 3 and turn off **Use the t distribution**. The z interval is too narrow and coverage drops well below 95%. Turn t back on and it recovers.
4. Switch the population to **Clicks** with a small sample. Sometimes nobody clicks, the sample's spread is zero, and the interval collapses to a point that misses. This is why proportions get their own interval methods.
5. Move **Confidence level** to 80% and then 99%. Coverage follows, and so does the width.

## The maths

<Formulas>

<Formula label="Interval for a mean">

$$
\bar{x} \pm t_{n-1,\,1-\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

</Formula>

<Formula label="Large-sample version">

$$
\bar{x} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}, \quad z_{0.975} \approx 1.96
$$

</Formula>

<Formula label="The guarantee">

$$
P\big(L \le \mu \le U\big) = 1 - \alpha \quad \text{over repeated samples}
$$

</Formula>

<Formula label="Width shrinks like">

$$
\text{width} \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

</Formula>

</Formulas>
