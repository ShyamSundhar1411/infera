---
summary: "The number of events in a fixed window of time or space, when events happen independently at a constant average rate λ."
---


## The story

Imagine events that arrive at random but at a steady average rate: emails landing in an inbox, clicks on a Geiger counter. Chop the window into a huge number of tiny slices, each of which almost never contains an event, and the total count is a Binomial with enormous $n$ and tiny $p$. In the limit you get the Poisson, with $P(X = k) = e^{-\lambda} \lambda^k / k!$.

Its defining quirk is that the mean and the variance are both equal to $\lambda$. Small $\lambda$ gives a right-skewed pile of bars near zero; as $\lambda$ grows the distribution becomes more symmetric and bell-shaped, with standard deviation $\sqrt{\lambda}$.

$$
P(X = k) = \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}

</Formula>

$$

</Formula>

<Formula label="Variance">

</Formula>

\lfloor \lambda \rfloor
$$

</Formula>

## Parameters

| Parameter | What it controls |
| --- | --- |
| $\lambda$ | The average number of events per window. It is both the mean and the variance, so larger $\lambda$ moves the distribution right and spreads it out. |

## Where it shows up

- The number of calls reaching a call centre in a ten-minute window.
- Typos per page in a long manuscript.
- Radioactive decays detected per second.
- Goals scored by a football team in a match.

## Try this

1. Press **Draw 1000** at $\lambda = 4$ and compare the sample mean and sample variance: both should land close to 4, the signature of a Poisson count.
2. Slide $\lambda$ from 0.5 up to 20 and watch the shape change from a lopsided pile at zero to an almost symmetric bell centred on 20 with $\sigma \approx 4.47$.
3. Set $\lambda = 1$ and shade the interval from 0 to 0: the shaded probability is $e^{-1} \approx 0.368$, the chance that nothing happens at all in a window where one event is expected.

## Related distributions

- It is the limit of the $\text{Binomial}(n, p)$ as $n \to \infty$ and $p \to 0$ with $np = \lambda$ fixed.
- In a Poisson process with rate $\lambda$, the waiting times between events are independent $\text{Exponential}(\lambda)$ variables.
- The sum of independent Poisson variables is Poisson, with the rates added together.
- For large $\lambda$ it is well approximated by a Normal with mean $\lambda$ and variance $\lambda$.
