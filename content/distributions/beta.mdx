## The story

Suppose you want to express what you believe about a coin's bias, a click-through rate or a conversion rate: some number between 0 and 1 that you do not know exactly. The $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ family can be flat, U-shaped, humped in the middle or piled up against either end, all controlled by two parameters. Its mean is $\alpha/(\alpha + \beta)$.

A useful way to read the parameters is as pseudo-counts: $\alpha$ behaves like a number of successes and $\beta$ like a number of failures. Start with a $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ belief about $p$, observe $s$ successes and $f$ failures, and your updated belief is exactly $\text{Beta}(\alpha + s, \beta + f)$. The larger $\alpha + \beta$, the more concentrated the curve, just as more data makes you more certain.

F(x) = I_x(\alpha, \beta)
$$

</Formula>

<Formula label="Expectation">

$$
\mathbb{E}[X] = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta}
$$

</Formula>

<Formula label="Variance">

$$
\operatorname{Var}(X) = \frac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha + \beta)^2 (\alpha + \beta + 1)}
$$

</Formula>

<Formula label="Mode">

$$
\frac{\alpha - 1}{\alpha + \beta - 2} \quad \text{for } \alpha, \beta > 1
$$

</Formula>

</Formulas>

## Parameters

| Parameter | What it controls |
| --- | --- |
| $\alpha$ | Pulls probability towards 1; think of it as prior successes. Raising $\alpha$ shifts the mean $\alpha/(\alpha + \beta)$ upwards. |
| $\beta$ | Pulls probability towards 0; think of it as prior failures. Raising both $\alpha$ and $\beta$ together narrows the curve without moving its centre much. |

## Where it shows up

- Uncertainty about a website's conversion rate in Bayesian A/B testing.
- The proportion of a day a machine spends idle.
- A batter's underlying hit rate, estimated from a season of at-bats.
- The fraction of a task completed by a deadline in project planning.

## Try this

1. Set $\alpha = \beta = 1$ to see a flat line, then lower both to 0.5. The curve becomes U-shaped: values near 0 and 1 are now more likely than values in the middle.
2. Play Bayesian updating: start at $\alpha = 1$, $\beta = 1$, then imagine 7 successes and 3 failures by setting $\alpha = 8$, $\beta = 4$. The mean moves to $8/12 \approx 0.667$ and the curve narrows. Shade from 0.5 to 1 to see how confident you now are that $p$ exceeds a half.
3. Compare $\alpha = \beta = 2$ with $\alpha = \beta = 10$. Both are centred on 0.5, but the standard deviation falls from about 0.224 to about 0.109. Press **Draw 1000** on each to see the difference in the histogram.

## Related distributions

- Beta(1, 1) is the Uniform on $[0, 1]$.
- It is the conjugate prior for the $p$ of a Bernoulli or Binomial: a Beta prior plus Binomial data gives a Beta posterior.
- The $j$-th smallest of $n$ independent Uniform(0, 1) draws follows $\text{Beta}(j, n - j + 1)$.
- If $X$ is $\text{Gamma}(\alpha, \theta)$ and $Y$ is $\text{Gamma}(\beta, \theta)$, independent, then $X/(X + Y)$ is $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$.
