summary: "A positive, right-skewed distribution whose logarithm is Normal. It arises when many independent factors multiply together."
---


## The story

The Normal comes from adding many small effects; the Log-normal comes from multiplying them. If a quantity grows by a random percentage again and again, its logarithm is a sum of many small pieces and so is roughly Normal, which makes the quantity itself Log-normal. Formally, $X = e^Y$ where $Y$ is Normal with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$.

Note that $\mu$ and $\sigma$ describe the underlying Normal, not $X$ itself. The median of $X$ is $e^\mu$, the mean is $e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$ and the mode is $e^{\mu - \sigma^2}$, so in this right-skewed distribution $\text{mode} < \text{median} < \text{mean}$. Increasing $\sigma$ stretches the right tail dramatically and pulls the mean further away from the median.

e^{\mu - \sigma^2}

## Parameters

| Parameter | What it controls |
| --- | --- |
| $\mu$ | The mean of $\ln X$. It sets the scale: the median of $X$ is $e^\mu$, and shifting $\mu$ by 1 multiplies every value by $e$. |
| $\sigma$ | The standard deviation of $\ln X$. It controls the skew: small $\sigma$ gives a near-symmetric hump, large $\sigma$ a sharp peak near zero with a long right tail. |

## Where it shows up

- Household incomes and personal wealth.
- Share prices under the standard Black-Scholes model.
- The sizes of particles produced by repeated grinding.
- Times taken to complete a task, which are positive with occasional very long durations.

## Try this

1. Set $\mu = 0$ and $\sigma = 1$ and shade from 0 to 1. The probability is exactly 0.5, because the median is $e^0 = 1$. Notice the mean marker sitting out at $e^{0.5} \approx 1.65$.
2. Hold $\mu = 0$ and move $\sigma$ from 0.1 up to 1.5. At small $\sigma$ the curve looks almost Normal; at large $\sigma$ the peak crowds towards zero while the tail and the mean run off to the right.
3. With $\sigma = 1.5$, switch to **Sample means** and compare $n = 5$ with $n = 50$. The averages stay visibly skewed for longer than with most distributions: the Central Limit Theorem always wins, but heavy skew slows it down.

## Related distributions

- If $X$ is $\text{Log-normal}(\mu, \sigma)$, then $\ln X$ is Normal with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$.
- Products of independent Log-normals are Log-normal, just as sums of independent Normals are Normal.
- Like the Gamma it is positive and right-skewed, but its right tail is heavier.
