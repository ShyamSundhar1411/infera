## The story

One Exponential wait is the time until the next event; add up $k$ independent Exponential waits, each with mean $\theta$, and you get the time until the $k$-th event. That sum is $\text{Gamma}(k, \theta)$. The definition extends smoothly to non-integer $k$, which makes the Gamma a general-purpose model for positive, skewed quantities.

The mean is $k\theta$ and the variance is $k\theta^2$. The shape $k$ controls the form: below 1 the density shoots up towards infinity at zero, at exactly 1 it is the Exponential, and above 1 it rises to a peak at $(k - 1)\theta$ before tailing off. As $k$ grows, the skew fades and the curve looks more and more Normal. The scale $\theta$ simply stretches the horizontal axis.

## Parameters

| Parameter | What it controls |
| --- | --- |
| $k$ | The shape: roughly, how many Exponential waits are being added up. Larger $k$ means a less skewed, more bell-like curve. |
| $\theta$ | The scale: the mean of each underlying Exponential wait. Doubling $\theta$ doubles the mean and quadruples the variance. |

## Where it shows up

- The total time to serve the next three customers in a queue.
- Monthly rainfall totals, which are positive and right-skewed.
- Insurance claim sizes.
- A prior for a Poisson rate or a Normal precision in Bayesian statistics.

## Try this

1. Fix $\theta = 1$ and sweep $k$ from 0.5 to 10. Watch the curve go from a spike at zero, through the Exponential at $k = 1$, to an almost symmetric hump with its mode at $k - 1$.
2. Set $k = 1.5$ and $\theta = 2$, then open the Chi-squared page with $k = 3$. The two curves are the same, because Chi-squared(3) is $\text{Gamma}(3/2, 2)$.
3. Choose $k = 2$, $\theta = 1.5$, press **Draw 1000** and check the sample mean against $k\theta = 3$ and the sample variance against $k\theta^2 = 4.5$.

## Related distributions

- The Exponential with rate $\lambda$ is Gamma with $k = 1$ and $\theta = 1/\lambda$.
- Chi-squared with $k$ degrees of freedom is $\text{Gamma}(k/2, 2)$.
- Independent Gammas with the same $\theta$ add: $\text{Gamma}(k_1, \theta) + \text{Gamma}(k_2, \theta)$ is $\text{Gamma}(k_1 + k_2, \theta)$.
- For large $k$ it is approximately Normal with mean $k\theta$ and variance $k\theta^2$.
