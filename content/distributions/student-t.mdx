## The story

When you standardise a sample mean using the sample standard deviation $S$ instead of the true $\sigma$, the extra uncertainty in $S$ makes extreme values more likely. William Gosset, writing as Student, worked out that for Normal data $(\bar{X} - \mu)/(S/\sqrt{n})$ follows a $t$ distribution with $\nu = n - 1$ degrees of freedom. Formally, $t$ is $Z/\sqrt{V/\nu}$ with $Z$ standard Normal and $V$ an independent $\text{Chi-squared}(\nu)$.

Small $\nu$ means heavy tails: at $\nu = 1$ the mean does not even exist, and the variance $\nu/(\nu - 2)$ is finite only when $\nu > 2$. As $\nu$ grows, $S$ becomes a reliable estimate of $\sigma$ and the $t$ distribution closes in on the standard Normal; by $\nu = 30$ the two are hard to tell apart by eye.

## Parameters

| Parameter | What it controls |
| --- | --- |
| $\nu$ | The degrees of freedom. Small $\nu$ gives heavy tails and frequent extreme values; large $\nu$ makes the curve almost exactly standard Normal. |

## Where it shows up

- One-sample and two-sample t-tests on small samples.
- Confidence intervals for a mean when $\sigma$ is unknown.
- Testing regression coefficients in linear models.
- Robust modelling of noisy data with occasional outliers, such as financial returns.

## Try this

1. Shade from $-1.96$ to $1.96$ at $\nu = 30$ and you get about 0.94, a little under the Normal's 0.95. Now set $\nu = 1$: the same interval holds only about 0.70, because so much probability has moved into the tails.
2. Set $\nu = 1$, switch to **Sample means** and increase $n$. The histogram of averages refuses to narrow: the mean of $n$ Cauchy draws is itself Cauchy, so the Central Limit Theorem does not apply when the variance is not finite.
3. Set $\nu = 3$ and press **Draw 1000** several times, clearing in between. The theoretical variance is 3, but the sample variance jumps around wildly as rare, huge draws come and go. Repeat at $\nu = 30$ and see how much more stable it is.

## Related distributions

- $t$ with $\nu$ degrees of freedom tends to the standard Normal as $\nu \to \infty$.
- It is built as $Z/\sqrt{V/\nu}$, where $Z$ is standard Normal and $V$ is an independent Chi-squared with $\nu$ degrees of freedom.
- With $\nu = 1$ it is the Cauchy distribution, which has no mean and no variance.
