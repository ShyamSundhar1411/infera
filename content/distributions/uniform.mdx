## The story

Pick a point at random on a ruler between $a$ and $b$, with no preference for any part. The chance of landing in any sub-interval depends only on its length: $P(c \le X \le d) = (d - c)/(b - a)$. The CDF is therefore a straight ramp rising from 0 at $a$ to 1 at $b$.

Its mean is the midpoint $(a + b)/2$ and its variance is $(b - a)^2/12$. The Uniform(0, 1) is also the raw material of simulation: computers generate uniform random numbers and transform them into everything else, for instance by feeding them through an inverse CDF.

## Parameters

| Parameter | What it controls |
| --- | --- |
| $a$ | The lower end of the interval. Keep it below $b$. |
| $b$ | The upper end of the interval. Wider intervals give a lower, flatter rectangle, since the total area must stay 1. |

## Where it shows up

- The output of a random number generator such as Math.random(), on $[0, 1)$.
- The rounding error when a measurement is rounded to the nearest unit, roughly uniform on $[-0.5, 0.5]$.
- The arrival time of a bus that comes once every ten minutes, if you turn up at a random moment.
- The angle at which a spun bottle comes to rest, uniform on $[0^\circ, 360^\circ)$.

## Try this

1. Set $a = 0$ and $b = 1$, then toggle to the CDF view: it is a straight line, so $P(X \le x)$ is simply $x$. Shade from 0.2 to 0.5 and confirm the probability is 0.3.
2. With $a = 0$ and $b = 1$, switch to **Sample means** with $n = 2$. The averages form a triangle, not a rectangle. Try $n = 3$ and then $n = 12$ and watch the Central Limit Theorem take over.
3. Press **Draw 1000** on Uniform(0, 1) and compare the sample variance with $1/12 \approx 0.0833$. Then widen $b$ to 4 and check that the variance grows to $16/12 \approx 1.33$, because it scales with the square of the width.

## Related distributions

- It is the continuous counterpart of the Discrete Uniform.
- Uniform(0, 1) is the Beta with $\alpha = \beta = 1$.
- If $U$ is Uniform(0, 1), then $-\ln(U)/\lambda$ is Exponential with rate $\lambda$; this is the inverse-CDF trick in action.
- The mean of $n$ independent Uniforms tends to a Normal as $n$ grows; even $n = 12$ already looks convincingly bell-shaped.
